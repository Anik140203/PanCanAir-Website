Heat pump vs boiler for Toronto buildings: the 2026 decision guide.

By Pankaj Oberoi·Owner & HVAC Engineer·Oct 21, 2026·11 min read

Building electrification is the biggest shift facing GTA property managers this decade, and it usually arrives as one question: when our boiler dies, do we replace it with another boiler, or switch to heat pumps? There's no single right answer — but there is a right way to decide. Here's the honest comparison for Toronto's climate.

How each one works, in one line

A boiler burns natural gas to make heat. A heat pump moves heat from outside air (or the ground, or water) into the building using electricity — and the same machine can run in reverse to cool. That one difference drives everything that follows.

The head-to-head comparison

FactorBoiler vs. Heat Pump
FuelGas vs. electricity

Cold-climate reality: can heat pumps handle Toronto winters?

This is the question every board asks, and the honest answer is: modern cold-climate heat pumps perform far better than the old reputation suggests, delivering useful heat well below freezing. But their efficiency does fall as it gets colder, and in Toronto's coldest stretches a supplementary heat source is often needed to carry peak demand.

That reality is why, for most GTA buildings, the practical path isn't "rip out the boiler and go all-electric overnight." It's a hybrid system.

THE PRAGMATIC ANSWER

For most existing GTA high-rises, the smart move is hybrid: a heat pump carries the building through shoulder seasons and milder weather (most of the year), while the gas boiler handles peak cold snaps. You cut carbon and gas use substantially without betting the building's heat on electrification in a single step — and you can electrify further over time as the grid and incentives evolve.

The cost question — why there's no simple winner

On upfront cost, boilers generally win, especially as a retrofit — heat pumps can require electrical service upgrades and more extensive work. On operating cost, it genuinely depends: heat pumps are extremely efficient (delivering 2–4 units of heat per unit of electricity), but Ontario electricity costs more per unit of energy than gas, so the comparison swings with utility rates, carbon pricing, and winter severity. On carbon, heat pumps win clearly on Ontario's relatively clean grid.

Anyone who gives you a flat "heat pumps always save money" or "boilers are always cheaper" is oversimplifying. The real answer requires running your building's actual loads, rates, and infrastructure — which is what an engineering assessment does.

Incentives: real, but moving

There are incentive programs for heat pumps and building electrification through federal, provincial, and utility channels — but they change frequently, and the 2026 landscape is different from two years ago. Because amounts and availability shift, confirm current programs before planning, and time any project to capture what's available. An HVAC partner who tracks these programs can help identify what applies to your specific building.

How to actually decide

When your boiler approaches end of life, work through these questions rather than defaulting to a like-for-like replacement:

  1. What's our carbon goal and timeline? If the board has committed to emissions reduction, that reshapes the decision.
  2. What's our distribution system? Hydronic buildings, forced-air, and in-suite systems each suit different electrification paths.
  3. What's our electrical capacity? A service upgrade can be a significant cost that changes the math.
  4. What incentives are available right now? These can materially shift the business case.
  5. Is a hybrid the pragmatic first step? For most, cutting gas use substantially with a hybrid beats waiting for a perfect all-electric solution.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are heat pumps good for cold climates like Toronto?

Modern cold-climate heat pumps maintain useful heating output well below freezing, far better than older models — but efficiency drops in deep cold, so a supplementary source is often needed at peak. For GTA buildings, hybrid systems (heat pump most of the year, gas boiler for peak cold) are usually the most practical path.

Is a heat pump cheaper to run than a gas boiler?

It depends on electricity vs. gas rates and the heat pump's efficiency. Heat pumps deliver 2–4 units of heat per unit of electricity, but Ontario electricity costs more per unit of energy than gas. The comparison shifts with rates, carbon pricing, and winter severity; carbon reduction is the clearest heat-pump win today.

Should a condo replace its boiler with a heat pump?

For most high-rises, a one-step switch is a major infrastructure project, not a swap. A hybrid or phased approach — adding heat pump capacity for milder periods while keeping gas for peak — is more common. The right answer depends on distribution, electrical capacity, and carbon goals, which an engineering assessment determines.

What incentives exist for building electrification in Ontario?

Programs exist through federal, provincial, and utility channels but change frequently. Because availability and amounts shift, confirm current programs before planning. A partner who tracks these can identify what applies to your building and time it to the capital plan.
