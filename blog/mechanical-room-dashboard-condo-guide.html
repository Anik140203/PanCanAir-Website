What a mechanical room dashboard does for your condo board.

By Pankaj Oberoi·Owner & HVAC Engineer·Oct 16, 2026·10 min read

For decades, the only way to know how a building's mechanical systems were doing was to walk down to the basement and read a screen — or wait for something to break. A mechanical room dashboard changes that: the whole plant, live, on your phone, with the problems flagged before residents feel them. Here's what it actually is, how it works, and whether your building needs one.

The problem a dashboard solves

Picture how most GTA condos manage mechanical equipment today. The boiler, chiller, cooling tower, and air handlers run in a locked room. A contractor visits periodically. Service records live in a binder, or an email thread, or someone's memory. When something goes wrong, the first signal is usually a resident complaint — "no heat on the 14th floor" — which means the problem is already affecting people by the time anyone knows.

A mechanical room dashboard flips that sequence. Instead of finding out about problems from residents, the property manager sees them forming in real time — and often gets them fixed before a single person is uncomfortable.

What a mechanical room dashboard actually is

In plain terms: a mechanical room dashboard is a web app that shows all of your building's mechanical equipment in one place, live, with the problems highlighted. A good one does four things:

1. Real-time equipment monitoring

It reads live data from each piece of equipment — boiler combustion efficiency, chiller temperatures, cooling tower water chemistry, pump status, generator readiness — and shows each system's health at a glance, usually with simple colour coding. Green is good, amber needs attention, red means act now.

2. Complete service history

Every maintenance visit, repair, and inspection is logged with date, technician, notes, and photos. No more binders, no more "when did we last service that chiller?" It's searchable in seconds, which matters enormously at AGM and reserve-fund-study time.

3. Alerts

When a reading drifts out of range or maintenance comes due, the dashboard sends an alert — to a phone, email, or team channel. The property manager finds out the moment something changes, not at the next scheduled visit.

4. Board-ready reports

One click produces a professional PDF of the building's mechanical status, service history, and upcoming needs — the kind of document that makes AGMs and reserve-fund conversations straightforward instead of stressful.

Dashboard vs. building automation system — what's the difference?

This confuses a lot of property managers, so here's the clean distinction:

Building Automation System (BAS)Mechanical Dashboard
Controls the equipmentMakes the equipment visible & manageable
Runs schedules & setpointsShows health scores & service history
Built for a controls engineerBuilt for a property manager
Lives on a screen in the basementLives on your phone
Raw data pointsClear status, alerts, reports

A BAS like a Tridium Niagara or Honeywell system runs the building. A dashboard sits on top of it and translates what it's doing into something a non-engineer can see and act on. You don't choose one or the other — the dashboard reads from the BAS.

"Do I have to replace my controls to get a dashboard?"

No — and this is the most important thing to understand before any vendor tells you otherwise. A well-designed dashboard connects to the systems you already have:

  • If your building has a BAS, the dashboard reads from it (via BACnet, oBIX, or a small read-only gateway) — no changes to your controls.
  • If your building is older and has no BAS, inexpensive wireless sensors can feed the dashboard the key readings instead.

Either way, there's no rip-and-replace. The dashboard adds visibility on top of your existing equipment. Be skeptical of any provider whose dashboard requires replacing your controls — that's a much bigger project being sold as a small one.

Why GTA boards are adopting dashboards now

Three forces are pushing this from "nice to have" to "expected":

  1. Liability and due diligence. Boards are increasingly aware that documented, photo-verified maintenance protects directors. A dashboard is a continuous due-diligence record.
  2. Aging buildings. The 2005–2015 condo boom means thousands of GTA buildings are now entering the equipment-replacement window. Boards need visibility to plan reserve funds instead of being surprised by failures.
  3. Energy costs. With HVAC the biggest operating line, boards want to see where energy goes — and dashboards make the waste visible and the savings trackable.

What to look for in a mechanical dashboard

If your building is evaluating one, these are the questions that separate a real tool from a pretty screen:

  • Does it connect to our existing BAS, or does it require new controls?
  • Does it log service history with photos, not just live readings?
  • Do alerts reach a phone in under a minute, or just sit on a screen?
  • Can it produce board-ready PDF reports in one click?
  • Is our data held with Canadian data residency?
  • Crucially: who fixes what the dashboard finds? A dashboard that flags a failing chiller but leaves you to find a contractor is half a solution.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a mechanical room dashboard?

A web app that gives a property manager a single, real-time view of all of a building's mechanical equipment — boilers, chillers, cooling towers, pumps, AHUs, generators — with health status, service history, photos, and alerts. The building's mechanical systems, on your phone instead of only in the basement.

How is a dashboard different from a building automation system?

A BAS (Niagara, Honeywell, etc.) controls the equipment — schedules and setpoints. A dashboard sits on top and makes that data understandable for a non-engineer: health scores, service history, phone alerts, and board reports. The BAS runs the building; the dashboard lets you see and manage it.

Do I need to replace my BAS to use a dashboard?

No. A good dashboard connects to your existing BAS via BACnet, oBIX, or a small read-only gateway. Older buildings without a BAS can use inexpensive wireless sensors. You keep your controls — the dashboard adds visibility, not a replacement project.
