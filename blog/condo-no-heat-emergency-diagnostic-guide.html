Condo has no heat? A 10-minute emergency diagnostic for PMs.

By Pankaj Oberoi· Owner & HVAC Engineer· Jun 22, 2026· 8 min read

It's 8pm on a Saturday in February. Residents are calling. The board chair is texting. Before you dispatch an emergency callout at $385/hour minimum, run this 10-step diagnostic. Half the time you'll catch the fix yourself; the other half, you'll save 30–60 minutes of tech time once they arrive.

Safety First If you smell gas, hear a sustained hissing near the boiler, or see scorch marks, leave the mechanical room immediately and call 911 + Enbridge (1-866-763-5427). Do not attempt diagnostic steps on a system that's showing signs of combustion failure.

What you need before you start

  • Building access keys (mechanical room, electrical room)
  • Flashlight
  • Phone with camera (for photographing fault codes)
  • Building Automation System (BAS) login if you have one
  • Most recent boiler inspection report for reference

The 10-step diagnostic

1 ~30 seconds

Confirm whether it's building-wide or zone-specific

Call back two of the complaining residents and ask them to confirm: are all their radiators cold, or just some? Are they on the same floor or different floors?

Different floors with full no-heat = building-wide. Same floor or same stack only = zone-specific. This determines whether you're chasing a central plant issue or a distribution issue, and where you walk first.

2 ~1 minute

Walk into the mechanical room and look at the boilers

Each boiler will have a display panel showing one of four states: STANDBY (sitting idle waiting for a call), FIRING (currently running), LOCKOUT (faulted and refusing to fire), or a numerical fault code.

If all boilers show LOCKOUT or a fault code — you have a central plant problem. Continue to Step 3.

If boilers are firing but heat isn't reaching units — skip to Step 4 (circulator pumps).

3 ~1 minute

Photograph every fault code

Take a clear photo of each boiler's display showing the fault code. Common codes you'll see:

  • E04 / F32 / "Low water" — make-up water failure or system leak (go to Step 5)
  • E22 / F22 / "Ignition lockout" — flame failed to establish; usually gas supply or igniter
  • E33 / "Flue gas" — exhaust path blocked (snow on rooftop intake/vent is common in winter)
  • "Sensor fault" — failed temperature sensor; tech needs to attend

Photograph everything before resetting. You'll need the codes for the tech.

4 ~1 minute

Confirm circulator pumps are running

Put your hand on each circulator pump housing. A running pump vibrates and is warm. A failed pump is silent and cold to the touch.

If pumps are off but should be running: check the pump breakers in the electrical room. A tripped breaker is common.

If pumps are running but flow isn't reaching units: probable cause is an air-locked loop or closed isolation valve. Tech required.

5 ~2 minutes

Check system pressure on the boiler gauge

Each boiler has a pressure gauge on the front (psi). Normal cold operating pressure: 12–25 psi.

If pressure reads below 10 psi — your make-up water has failed. The boiler will lock out on low water as a safety measure. Continue to Step 6.

If pressure reads above 30 psi — the expansion tank has likely failed (waterlogged). Tech required, but the boiler will run safely on the relief valve in the short term.

6 ~1 minute

Check the make-up water valve

The make-up water valve is the brass valve on a small pipe coming off the cold-water main into the boiler loop. It should be partially open (handle parallel to pipe = open; perpendicular = closed).

If the valve is closed: open it. System pressure will start to rise within 2–3 minutes. Once pressure reaches 15 psi, you can reset the boilers.

If the valve is open but pressure isn't rising: there's a leak somewhere in the system. Tech required.

7 ~1 minute

For zone-specific issues, check zone valves and aquastat

If only certain stacks or floors are cold, the issue is usually in distribution. Locate the zone valve panel (often in a sub-mechanical closet near the affected stack). Each zone valve should be opening when the thermostat calls for heat.

Listen for the valve motor clicking and the actuator turning. A stuck or failed zone valve will require tech replacement, but you've now isolated the problem.

8 ~2 minutes

Check the Building Automation System (BAS)

Log into your BAS (Honeywell, Siemens, Delta, Distech, etc.) and check:

  • Is the heating schedule active? (Sometimes a schedule gets accidentally overridden to "off")
  • Are setpoints reasonable? (A staff member may have set them to 50°F for "energy saving")
  • Are there any active alarms?
  • Is any mode override active? ("Manual", "Off", "Holiday")

Clear any incorrect overrides. Many no-heat calls turn out to be BAS schedule issues, not equipment failures.

9 ~30 seconds

For penthouse-fed buildings, confirm MAU operation

If your high-rise uses a penthouse-mounted Make-Up Air Unit (MAU) for corridor pressurization, walk up and confirm it's running. A failed MAU often presents as "no heat in upper floors" because the heating coil inside the MAU isn't transferring heat to incoming air.

10 ~1 minute

Document everything and call dispatch

You should now have:

  • Photo of every boiler fault code
  • Reading of current system pressure
  • Confirmation of which pumps are running / not running
  • Status of make-up water valve
  • BAS schedule / setpoint status

When you call your contractor's emergency line, lead with this information. A tech who arrives knowing the fault code, pressure reading, and confirmed pump status can often have heat restored in 30–60 minutes instead of 2–3 hours.

What you can fix yourself, and what you can't

Things a property manager can safely do without violating TSSA regulations:

  • Reset a boiler after a lockout (press the reset button)
  • Open the make-up water valve to restore system pressure
  • Reset tripped circuit breakers
  • Clear BAS schedule overrides
  • Document fault codes for the tech

Things that require licensed personnel under Ontario TSSA regulations:

  • Any work involving gas connections or combustion components
  • Pressure vessel work (boiler internals, expansion tank replacement)
  • Refrigerant work on chillers or heat pumps
  • Anything requiring entry into the firebox or burner assembly

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the most common cause of no heat in a Toronto condo?

In GTA high-rises, the top three causes of building-wide no-heat are: boiler lockout (often from low water pressure or flue-gas issues), circulator pump failure, and make-up water valve failure. Zone-specific outages are usually controls — failed zone valve, broken aquastat, or BAS override.

Can a property manager fix no heat without calling a contractor?

For some issues yes — resetting a boiler after a lockout, opening a manual make-up water valve, or clearing a BAS override. For anything involving combustion, refrigerant, or pressure vessels, you must call a licensed contractor. TSSA regulations in Ontario require licensed personnel for boiler service.

How long does a no-heat repair take?

For common faults: simple resets and pressure restoration take 30–60 minutes. Pump replacement is typically 2–4 hours. Boiler component failures can take 1–3 days if parts need to be sourced. Doing the 10-step diagnostic before dispatch saves significant time.
