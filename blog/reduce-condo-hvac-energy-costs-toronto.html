9 ways to cut condo HVAC energy costs in Toronto — without touching comfort.

By Pankaj Oberoi·Owner & HVAC Engineer·Oct 16, 2026·12 min read

HVAC is the single biggest operating cost in almost every GTA high-rise — often more than the building's entire staffing budget. The good news: most buildings are leaving 10–20% of that bill on the table in settings nobody has looked at in years. Here are the nine levers, ranked by payback, that cut the number without a resident ever noticing.

Before the list, one principle: the cheapest energy savings are almost always on the control side, not the equipment side. New condensing boilers and high-efficiency chillers save real money, but they're six-figure capital projects. The measures that pay back in months are about running the equipment you already own more intelligently. We've ordered this list roughly by payback speed — fastest first.

1. Control recommissioning (fastest payback)

Every building's control settings drift. Schedules get overridden and never reset, setpoints get nudged during a complaint and forgotten, sensors fall out of calibration, valves stick half-open. Over a few years the plant ends up fighting itself — heating and cooling in the same shoulder season, running full-tilt when the building is empty.

A control recommissioning is a technician systematically correcting all of it: schedules, setpoints, sensor calibration, stuck actuators. It typically costs a few thousand dollars and pays back within the first billing cycle. If you do nothing else on this list, do this.

2. Optimal start / stop

Most buildings start the heating or cooling plant on a fixed schedule — say, ramping up at 5 AM so suites are comfortable by 7. But buildings don't respond to a schedule; they respond to physics. A well-insulated building on a mild morning might reach target in 30 minutes, not two hours. Optimal start learns the building's actual response and starts the plant as late as possible while still hitting comfort on time. Pure runtime removed, comfort untouched.

3. Chilled-water and hot-water reset

Chilled-water reset raises the chilled-water setpoint when full cooling isn't needed — on a mild day the chiller doesn't have to produce 42°F water when 46°F keeps everyone comfortable, and every degree of reset meaningfully cuts compressor energy. Hot-water (outdoor-air) reset does the mirror image for heating: the boiler supply temperature tracks the outdoor temperature instead of running flat-out all season. Both are classic ASHRAE measures that save continuously.

4. Weather-responsive heating

Tie the heating plant to the forecast, not just the current outdoor temperature. If a 9°C warm-up is coming tomorrow afternoon, the plant can ease back in advance rather than heating a building that's about to warm up on its own. In Toronto's swing seasons — October–November and March–April — this captures savings a fixed schedule can't.

5. Peak-demand shaving (the Global Adjustment lever)

This is the big one for large buildings in Ontario. A significant slice of a commercial hydro bill is the Global Adjustment, allocated based on a building's demand during the province's five highest peak hours of the year. Pre-cooling the building before a forecast peak and shedding non-essential load during it can trim that allocation — and because the charge is large, the savings can be one of the biggest single line items you can move.

THE CATCH

Peak-demand shaving only works if someone is watching the forecast and the grid signals every single day and acting at the right hour. No property manager can do that manually across a portfolio. This is exactly where continuous monitoring and automation earn their keep — the system watches the peaks so nobody has to.

6. Variable-frequency drives (VFDs) on pumps and fans

A pump or fan running at a fixed speed is like driving with the gas pedal floored and controlling speed with the brake. VFDs let motors slow down when full flow isn't needed — and because fan and pump energy scales with the cube of speed, even a modest speed reduction cuts energy dramatically. Retrofitting VFDs onto constant-speed pumps and fans is a mid-cost project with a typical 2–4 year payback.

7. Economizer / free cooling

On cool days, why run the chiller at all when the outdoor air is already cold enough to cool the building? A water-side or air-side economizer uses that free cooling and shuts the compressor off. In Toronto's climate there are hundreds of economizer-eligible hours a year — a properly functioning (and properly maintained) economizer harvests them automatically.

8. Cooling tower optimization

A clean, well-treated cooling tower lets the chiller run cooler condenser water, which directly improves chiller efficiency — often 1–2% per degree of condenser-water temperature reduction. Pair that with cycles-of-concentration optimization on the water side and you cut both the energy bill and the water bill at once. (More on the water side in our cooling tower compliance guide.)

9. Equipment upgrades (highest cost, real long-term savings)

When equipment reaches end of life, replacing it with high-efficiency models — condensing boilers, modern chillers, EC fan motors — delivers 15–25% savings on that system. These are capital projects, so the right move is to plan them into the reserve fund on the equipment's natural replacement cycle rather than as emergencies, and let the control-side measures above carry the savings in the meantime.

What this adds up to for a real building

Here's the honest math for a 240-unit GTA high-rise spending roughly $220,000 a year on HVAC energy:

That 10–20% is achievable without replacing a single piece of equipment and without a comfort trade-off. The barrier isn't knowledge — these measures are decades old. The barrier is that no human can watch the controls, the weather, and the grid every hour across a building, which is why so much of this saving sits uncaptured.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can a condo realistically cut its HVAC energy bill?

A GTA high-rise that hasn't optimized its controls can typically cut 10–20% through control-side measures alone — reset, optimal start, outdoor-air reset, peak-demand shaving — with no equipment replacement and no comfort trade-off. On a $220,000/year HVAC energy bill, that's $22,000–$44,000 a year.

What is a demand charge and why does it matter in Ontario?

Large Ontario buildings pay partly on peak demand, including the Global Adjustment, allocated by demand during the province's five highest peak hours of the year. Pre-cooling before those peaks and shedding load during them reduces that charge — often one of the biggest line items on a commercial hydro bill.

Does HVAC energy optimization reduce resident comfort?

Done properly, no. The best savings change equipment runtime, not suite temperature — starting the plant later, raising chilled-water temperature on mild days, easing boilers before a warm-up. Comfort complaints rise only when optimization is done crudely without monitoring.

What is the fastest HVAC energy saving a condo can implement?

Control recommissioning — correcting drifted schedules, setpoints, and stuck valves/sensors. It typically costs a few thousand dollars and delivers savings within the first billing cycle, often paying back in under a year.
